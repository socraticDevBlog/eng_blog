Denis Villeneuve's *Sicario* (2015) is a police thriller, and what is sometimes called a neo-Western. It follows the hunt for a Mexican drug trafficker by a special unit made up of agents from the FBI, the CIA and foreign governments. The film is seen chiefly through the experience of FBI agent Kate Macer, played by Emily Blunt.

What interested me most was the moral injury this fictional character suffers. In Kate's case, the damage leaves visible physical traces. Watching her decline helps us, even when we approach a Hollywood film primarily as entertainment, grasp the seriousness of what she is experiencing. As a viewer interested in human behavior, though neither a psychologist nor an expert in moral philosophy, I found myself compelled to think about it further.

**Spoiler warning:** This article discusses several important plot points in *Sicario*, including Kate Macer's development and the film's ending.

The film opens with an FBI tactical team raiding a house under construction in a new development set, quite literally, in the desert. After neutralizing the guards, the agents discover the remains of a brutal massacre. That discovery prompts the federal government to establish the special mission in which Kate Macer, the film's central character, will take part.

### Moral injury or the loss of innocence

The loss of innocence is a turning point in human life. It is commonly understood as the passage from childhood to adulthood and psychological maturity. Without making a value judgment, we might describe it as the end of naivete. We learn that the world around us is not always good, fair or magical. We discover that people we trust can hurt us by lying to us or betraying us. Our culture generally treats this transition as part of growing up, and therefore as something ultimately positive.

Villeneuve's film pushes that idea further. It shows how an FBI agent who encounters human horror as part of her daily work can suffer another fall: an initiation into the hidden forces that govern the world.

### Professional competence as armor

Kate Macer is introduced as a woman wholly devoted to her work. Unlike her more experienced colleagues, she shows none of the familiar cynicism that can follow disappointed ambitions. She trusts her superiors, the protocol and the justice system. Within a few days, however, her reassuring universe of certainties, along with her self-image as a good person, is demolished.

Her professional competence initially functions as armor, something that should protect her from the world. But when she is drawn into an unprecedented crisis involving people with enormous power to act, she is quickly admitted to a circle of insiders. The conduct she witnesses and the actions in which she takes part become a source of moral injury.

Her superiors and peers know the system's darker side. They protect themselves with a disciplined stoicism, or with cynicism. Kate, by contrast, has chosen idealism and trust in her organization. When the system dismisses her objections and neutralizes her resistance, she experiences that failure as a personal betrayal.

### Incremental contamination

Kate's loss of innocence is gradual. As in real life, critical situations do not arrive with a neatly drawn map or an explicit briefing. Contamination usually takes the form of a succession of troubling events and realizations. Like a frog placed in water that is heated by degrees, the victim may not recognize the danger until escape has become difficult.

Left to herself, she tries to understand what is happening and to rationalize it. Each step appears manageable on its own. Together, the steps transform her relationship with the institution and with herself.

### Somatization: the body records the insult

Somatization has been given several medical interpretations. In general, it refers to the physical expression of a psychological problem or conflict.

The body is often the first place where moral injury becomes apparent. It may show up as insomnia, loss of appetite, hypervigilance, nausea, irritability or detachment.

I am not a physician, but I believe there is some truth in the popular advice to listen to one's body. Such symptoms, visible both to the person experiencing them and to those around them, can signal an inner disturbance that deserves attention.

> "Somatization implies a tendency to experience and communicate psychological distress in the form of somatic symptoms and to seek medical help for them. So defined, it is neither a disorder nor a diagnostic category but a generic term for a set of experimental, cognitive, and behavioral characteristics of patients who complain of physical symptoms in the absence of relevant medical findings."

### Structure and dissociation

One possible benefit of losing one's innocence is psychological maturity. For a competent employee who does not understand the hidden workings of an organization, successes and failures are attributed to personal ability and job performance. The employee feels responsible and sees a direct connection between work, recognition and results.

Kate initially retains her good conscience by following the orders and procedures of her police department. She is then invited to join the Joint Task Force, ostensibly to respond to the crimes she helped uncover. Finally, by placing her in situations where she must kill to protect her own life, the system makes her complicit in its operations.

In other words, the structure absorbs her. She becomes one of its moving parts. For someone with a highly conscientious personality, that assimilation results in the loss of her original innocence and the realization that she cannot return to the person she was.

The first consequence is a crisis of professional confidence. The moral constraints she values appear to prevent her from completing her mission.

Yet the organization, through her superior, assures her that her professional abilities are not in question. The organization is simply built this way. No director can openly describe the system's amorality and expect to remain in office. So the institution falls silent. Kate must abandon the hope of restoring moral order. The structure ignores and neutralizes dissent.

> "A structure is a stable arrangement of relations, norms, and powers that goes beyond individuals, but determines their choices, thoughts, and actions [...] In the case of cultic abuses or totalitarian regimes, this 'efficiency' becomes absolute: the structure takes over all of the individual's rules and logical resources to strip them of any capacity for autonomous action." ### Good conscience and the price of the ability to act

Initiation, loss of innocence and organizational structures are closely connected to environments of power. We tell ourselves that you cannot make an omelet without breaking eggs. Or, as French President François Mitterrand is reputed to have said:

> "Great fortunes are not made along the paths of virtue."

Matt Graver, the CIA officer played by Josh Brolin, and Alejandro Gillick, the hitman linked to the Medellin cartel and played by Benicio del Toro, have both internalized the structure. Graver wears beach sandals to a meeting with FBI officials, while Gillick sleeps through an important group briefing. They understand the system and know how to distinguish what matters from what does not.

Professional appearance and conformity to a dress code are not what the structure rewards. Remaining calm in critical situations and regulating one's energy are.

### Recognizing the possibility of thought reform

The passage from childhood to adulthood generally involves the loss of youthful ideals. It is usually regarded as difficult but positive.

The same process, however, can be exploited by illicit forces such as cults or totalitarian governments. These groups systematically challenge a person's identity: their beliefs, their memories and their sense of worth. A victim may be kept in a chaotic environment, where confusion weakens the ability to act. Eventually, the individual may be broken, and submission to the cult or totalitarian structure may appear to be the only way out.

The lesson is that no one is entirely immune to attempts at thought reform. By comparing their own experiences with Kate Macer's journey, some readers may recognize moments in which individuals or institutions tried to manipulate their thinking.

### Conclusion

The moral dilemma I identified in Denis Villeneuve's *Sicario* offers fertile ground for reflection. It resembles the archetype of the Fall: the loss of innocence and the realization that no all-powerful authority can protect us from life's vicissitudes. This awakening is often treated as a necessary evil.

I also see the "structure" as an inescapable reality that imposes itself on us. My argument is not that we should abandon our moral convictions, but that we should learn to recognize how structures operate. Understanding them can help us acquire the skills needed to act more effectively in the world.

I do not mean to suggest that abandoning one's convictions is justified in difficult circumstances. Rather, I hope this reflection shows how systems of control can shape our lives, and how recognizing their influence can help reduce their hold over us. That recognition begins with a sound distinction between what lies within our control and what does not.
